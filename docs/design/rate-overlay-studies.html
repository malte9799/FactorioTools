Bottleneck Overlay Studies
FactorioTools · Rate calculator redesign

Put the rate calculator on the factory floor

The side panel lists everything and explains nothing. These five studies move the numbers onto the blueprint itself and run a tick-by-tick simulation underneath, so a bottleneck shows up where it actually is: on a lane, an inserter, or a machine. Every plate below is live and shares one running simulation.

The test build

A fast (red) belt enters from the left carrying iron on its left lane and copper on its right, 15/s each. A splitter sends half of it up to the bus and half down a feed belt that runs past four assembling machine 2s making iron gears. Each one needs 3.00 iron/s; each is fed by one fast inserter that tops out around 2.31/s. Gears leave on the far lane of an output belt that dives under two pipes and exits right.

The build has two real problems and one red herring, all only visible at lane level. Use the what-if buttons above to fix them and watch every plate react.

A

Signal layer

hover a machine, belt or inserter

The build stays the hero. The overlay adds two things: a ring around every machine that fills with its uptime, and two thin strips on every belt tile, one per lane.

Hidden
Every number. The map reads as colour first: green is fine, yellow is an arm, red is supply.
Revealed by
Hovering. The card names the limit in words ("limited by its inserter: 2.31/s of 3.00/s") instead of listing rates.
Watch out
Lane strips get busy on a 2,000-belt blueprint. Fade strips on lanes with no downstream demand.

B

Flow ribbons

width = items per second

Belts turn into light. Each lane becomes a ribbon whose width is its measured throughput and whose colour is its item. Splitters fork ribbons, undergrounds arc over what they cross, inserters are short arcs that pulse on every swing.

Hidden
The entity art, dimmed to a silhouette. What's left is where volume goes and where it thins out.
Revealed by
Reading widths. The feed ribbon visibly narrows at each inserter and runs out before the last machine.
Watch out
Beautiful but less exact. Best as a view you toggle (F), not the default.

C

Semantic zoom

zoom decides the detail

No new controls. The scroll wheel people already use decides how much they see. Far out, machines collapse into status blocks and a group gets one summary. In the middle, lanes and rates. Close up, the real items, inserter hands and input buffers.

Hidden
Everything below the current zoom's level of detail, behind a gesture that costs nothing.
Revealed by
Zooming toward what looks wrong. The overview tells you where; the detail tells you why.
Watch out
Thresholds need tuning per blueprint size, and labels need collision handling at the middle level.

D

Jam trace

pick an issue

Start from the answer. A short list of what's wrong, ranked by lost output. Pick one and the rest of the build fades while a line runs from the symptom upstream to the cause, with the measured rate at every step.

Hidden
Everything that is fine. This replaces the old panel's job: it listed every item; this lists what costs you output.
Watch out
The cause attribution has to be right. One wrong diagnosis and people stop trusting the list.

Inspection lens

move over the build

The build stays untouched except for small status lights. A magnifier follows the cursor and shows the lane-level truth underneath it, with readouts on its rim like a gauge bezel.

Hidden
All detail, everywhere except the one spot you're looking at.
Revealed by
Pointing. Hold Alt to summon it, release to put it away.
Watch out
Overlaps with semantic zoom, and it only answers "what's here", never "where's the problem".

Recommendation

Combine three of them. Each one covers a different question, and they share one render pass and one simulation.

A · Signal layer

The always-on layer

Rings and lane strips are on whenever the calculator is open. Cheap, calm, and readable in the colours Factorio players already know.

C · Semantic zoom

Controls the detail

Decides how much of A is drawn. Zoomed out it collapses into group summaries; zoomed in it shows items and inserter hands. It needs no new controls.

D · Jam trace

The entry point

Replaces the sidebar. One chip in the corner says "3 issues · −2.4 gears/s". Opening it gives the ranked list, the trace and the what-if fixes.

Where the old panel's contents go

  • Products and ingredients shrink to a strip of item chips along the top edge. Clicking a chip lights up that item's lanes on the map.
  • Timescale, multiplier, target move into a compact settings popover on that strip.
  • Machine groups become the zoomed-out group summaries from C.
  • Warnings join the issue list in D, ranked alongside bottlenecks.
  • Blueprint edges get port tabs where belts enter and leave. You set what flows in there, and each one shows its measured rate.

What to leave out

  • B · Flow ribbons as a second view mode behind a key, once lane-item inference exists. The data is the same, so it's cheap later.
  • E · Lens is dropped. C answers the same question without a special tool.

The simulation underneath

The current bottleneck check only looks at what's physically next to a machine. A 1:1 lane simulation replaces it and runs headless in a Web Worker, so the renderer only reads positions each frame.

Mechanics to model

  • Transport lines. Merge connected belt pieces into per-lane lines the way the game does. Items keep 0.25-tile spacing, and a line moves as one block until it hits a gap.
  • Curves and side-loading. The inner and outer lanes of a curve have different lengths. A side-load only feeds the lane it hits.
  • Splitters. Alternate items per lane, keep lanes separate, and honour input/output priority and filters from the blueprint.
  • Undergrounds. Lanes continue through the tunnel at the tunnel's length. A side-load onto an entrance only reaches one lane, the classic lane-filter trick.
  • Inserters. Real pickup and drop vectors, rotation and extension speed, hand size with research, chasing items on a moving belt, dropping on the far lane, and the machine's automatic insertion limits.
  • Machines. Crafting progress, ingredient buffers, output full, modules and beacons from the existing calc/effects.ts.

What the data pipeline needs to add

  • Belt speed (only throughput exists today), underground max_distance, and splitter prototypes.
  • Inserter rotation_speed, extension_speed, pickup_position, insert_position, and hand-size bonuses.
  • Item stack sizes and the crafting machines' ingredient limits.

Open questions

  • What's on a belt? A blueprint doesn't say. Infer it downstream from producers and upstream from what the inserters feed, then let the ports settle the rest.
  • When is it steady? Warm up headless at 100×, then declare steady state once the output rates stop drifting.
  • Scale. Simulate the selection only on huge prints, and share one line model with the renderer's existing beltGraph.ts.
PHASE 1Pipeline data, then belts, lanes, splitters and undergrounds in a new DOM-free packages/sim, with tests.
PHASE 2Inserters and machines. Check the results against rates measured in-game on a set of reference prints.
PHASE 3Signal layer and semantic zoom in the renderer, plus port tabs and the time strip.
PHASE 4Issue ranking, trace drawing and what-if edits that re-run the simulation.
PHASE 5Flow ribbons as a second view. Remove the old side panel.

Your call

Does A + C + D feel right, or would you rather lead with a different one? I'd start with phase 1: the simulation core is the foundation for every option, and its correctness is testable on its own.
